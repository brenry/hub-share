# Library — reference books

Books supplied for research sessions (R-notes) to read and cite by page. Read-only for every session;
nothing here ships (`docs/` never ships, CLAUDE.md). New books: `Author-Year-Short-Title.pdf`, marked PARTIAL or
SUMMARY when they are not the whole book, with a line below.

| File | What it is | Useful for |
|---|---|---|
| `Grindstaff-2002-The-Money-Shot.pdf` | Laura Grindstaff, *The Money Shot: Trash, Class, and the Making of TV Talk Shows*, University of Chicago Press, 2002. Complete, 330 pages, text. | How a daytime television interview program with a live audience is built: segments between breaks and the "money shot"; guests brought on by segment; the audience's seating, warm-up and reaction shots, extra reactions taped after and inserted in editing (pp. 128, 130); the control room during a taping (pp. 12-13). No camera directions. |
| `Murch-2001-In-the-Blink-of-an-Eye-2nd-ed.pdf` | Walter Murch, *In the Blink of an Eye*, 2nd ed., Silman-James Press. Complete, 88 pages, text (ebook). | Where and why to cut: the Rule of Six ("The Rule of Six" chapter: emotion, story, rhythm, eye-trace, planarity, 3-D space, sacrificed from the bottom up); cutting where a viewer would blink ("Why Do Cuts Work?", "A Galaxy of Winking Dots"). The chapter's table of weights is not in the text layer. |
| `Katz-1991-Film-Directing-Shot-by-Shot.pdf` | Steven D. Katz, *Film Directing Shot by Shot: Visualizing from Concept to Screen*, Michael Wiese Productions, 1991. Complete, 386 pages, scanned with a text layer (OCR). | Storyboards (ch. 3, p. 23); framing heights and the cut on the look (ch. 6, pp. 121-123); editing, temporal connections (ch. 7, p. 145); staging dialogue: two players (ch. 9, p. 173), three (ch. 10, p. 195), four or more (ch. 11, p. 209); mobile staging, depth, angles, framings, point of view (chs. 12-16); pan, crane, tracking (chs. 17-19). |
| `McKee-2016-Dialogue.pdf` | Robert McKee, *Dialogue: The Art of Verbal Action for Page, Stage, and Screen*, Twelve, 2016. Complete, 279 pages, text. | Writing the exchange: dialogue as action, every line an inner action that acts on the other; the six tasks of dialogue (each line an action, beats building, exposition carried in the lines, a verbal style per role, progressive flow, authenticity); desire, scene intention and beats of action and reaction; Part Two's flaws and fixes; Part Four parses scenes beat by beat. |
| `Holman-2010-Sound-for-Film-and-Television-3rd-ed.pdf` | Tomlinson Holman, *Sound for Film and Television*, 3rd ed., Focal Press, 2010. Complete, 263 pages, text. About half is recording hardware (microphones, sync, analog/digital transfer, masters): not needed here. | The sound engineer's parts: speech intelligibility and the cocktail-party effect (binaural discrimination), ch. 2, pp. 32-37; production mixing: level setting, dialog overlaps, crowd scenes, ch. 7, pp. 117-118; sound design styles and conventions, ch. 10, pp. 145-151; dialog editing and multi-camera television comedy, ch. 11, pp. 157-166; mixing, ch. 12, p. 171. Little on today's loudness standards (LUFS/LKFS); the 2010 text predates their wide use. |
| `Zettl-1976-Television-Production-Handbook-3rd-ed.pdf` | Herbert Zettl, *Television Production Handbook*, 3rd ed., Wadsworth, 1976. Complete, 547 pages, scanned with a text layer (OCR). The analog multi-camera studio of its era. | The TV studio: switching, or instantaneous editing, and switching principles (ch. 10, pp. 259-276); directing: visualization and picturization, script formats, rehearsal, on the air (ch. 15, pp. 401-457); audio (ch. 7, p. 145); lighting (chs. 5-6); set design (ch. 12, p. 319); talent (ch. 13, p. 357). Interviews and panels throughout (55 and 33 mentions). |
