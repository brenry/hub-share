## 1. Coverage — the shots a dialogue scene is filmed in

1.1 **Coverage, defined.** "the amount and kind of footage shot used to capture a scene in filmmaking and video
production." Coverage exists because "the long take master shot looks static and stagey to modern audiences."
— Wikipedia, "Camera coverage", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_coverage (cites Ascher & Pincus, *The
Filmmaker's Handbook*, 2007; Brown, *Cinematography: Theory and Practice*, 2016; LoBrutto, *The Art of Motion
Picture Editing*, 2012; Mamet, *On Directing Film*, 1991; Rosenberg, *The Healthy Edit*, 2018).

1.2 **The master is shot first, the rest must match it.** "The scene is shot from beginning to end, usually but
not always using a wide shot, to create the 'master shot'." "The master shot is filmed first, since coverage must
match what occurs in the master." "All of these shots must obey the 180-degree rule." Call-and-answer shots use the
same lens size and focus distance (paraphrase of the page). — Wikipedia, "Camera coverage" (as 1.1).

1.3 **Master shot.** "a film recording of an entire dramatized scene, start to finish," filmed "from a camera angle
that keeps all the players in view"; typically a long shot; "the foundation of what is called camera coverage"
(two- or three-person groupings, close-ups, inserts of props). It "can sometimes perform a double function as an
establishing shot." From the 1960s–70s scenes are no longer built around the master as before (paraphrase).
— Wikipedia, "Master shot", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_shot (cites Ascher & Pincus, *The Filmmaker's
Handbook*, 1999, p. 227).

1.4 **Establishing shot.** It "sets up, or establishes, the context for a scene", shows "the relationship between
its important figures and objects", usually a long or extreme-long shot "at the beginning of a scene".
— Wikipedia, "Establishing shot", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Establishing_shot (cites Brown, *Cinematography:
Theory and Practice*, 2012; Boggs, *The Art of Watching Films*, 1996).
"The establishing shot is one that provides a view of all the space in which the action is occurring." "The
establishing shot can be used at any time as a reestablishing shot", which "might be necessary when a complex
sequence of cuts may have served to disorient the viewer." — Wikipedia, "Continuity editing",
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuity_editing (cites Rosenberg, *The Healthy Edit*, 2010; Bordwell & Thompson,
*Film Art*, 2006).

1.5 **Start the scene on the master; save the close-up.** "it's always a good idea to start the scene with the
master shot" (it establishes geography and orients the characters before the scene cuts closer — paraphrase).
Close-ups: "save it for the most emotional moments of the scene." — Kyle DeGuzman, "How to Edit a Dialogue Scene —
Tips and Techniques for Editors", StudioBinder, 2023-08-06, https://studiobinder.com/blog/how-to-edit-a-dialogue-scene

1.6 **Shot / reverse shot.** "a film technique where one character is shown looking at another character", the
other then shown looking back; "It is an example of an eyeline match"; because the actors seem to face each other,
viewers "assume that they are looking at each other." — Wikipedia, "Shot/reverse shot",
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shot/reverse_shot
It is "most often used for dialogue scenes," and "will often use over-the-shoulder shots"; "The standard operating
procedure for shot reverse shot is to use matching shots." — SC Lannom, "Shot Reverse Shot: Reaction Shots,
Cutaways, and Coverage", StudioBinder, https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/shot-reverse-shot-cutaways-coverage/

1.7 **Over-the-shoulder (OTS).** "the camera is placed above the back of the shoulder and head of a subject"; "most
commonly used to present conversational back and forth between two subjects"; each subject's OTS is cut in turn
for the dialogue and the reactions; the subject facing the camera is the focus; "'Matching' means the two people
involved remain on the same sides of the shot." — Wikipedia, "Over-the-shoulder shot",
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Over-the-shoulder_shot (cites Brown 2012; Mercado, *The Filmmaker's Eye*, 2013;
Bordwell & Thompson 2010; Proferes, *Film Directing Fundamentals*, 2012).

1.8 **The 180-degree rule.** "the camera should be kept on one side of an imaginary axis between two characters"
"so that the first character is always frame right of the second character"; crossing reverses their left-right
order and can disorient (paraphrase). — Wikipedia, "180-degree rule", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/180-degree_rule
(cites Proferes, *Film Directing Fundamentals*, 2005; Bordwell & Thompson, *Film Art*, 2012).

1.9 **The 30-degree rule.** "no edit should join two shots whose camera viewpoints are less than 30 degrees" apart.
— Wikipedia, "Continuity editing" (as 1.4). Otherwise the shot sizes must differ markedly, or another shot goes
between them (paraphrase). — Wikipedia, "Over-the-shoulder shot" (as 1.7).

1.10 **Eyeline match.** "When shooting a human subject, [the subject] can look towards the next subject to be cut to".
— Wikipedia, "Continuity editing" (as 1.4). The subject's gaze aligns with its target in the next shot
(paraphrase). — Wikipedia, "Over-the-shoulder shot" (as 1.7).

1.11 **Reaction shot.** "A reaction shot refers to when an action takes place on screen, and then cuts to a separate
shot" (of someone reacting — paraphrase). — SC Lannom, StudioBinder (as 1.6).
"Capturing a character's reaction can reveal emotion, establish conflict". — DeGuzman, StudioBinder (as 1.5).

1.12 **Cutaway.** "the interruption of a continuously filmed action by inserting a view of something else."
"The most common use of cutaway shots in dramatic films is to adjust the pace of the main action"; it can hide a
deleted part of the main shot. Question-and-answer exchanges: "On location, there is usually just one camera to film an interview";
"Cutaways to the interviewer, called noddies, can also be used to cover cuts." — Wikipedia, "Cutaway
(filmmaking)", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cutaway_(filmmaking) (cites Brown 2012).
"A cutaway can be a reaction shot, what a character is seeing, a piece of the environment, or an object."
— Wikipedia, "Camera coverage" (as 1.1).

1.13 **Insert.** "a shot of part of a scene as filmed from a different angle and/or focal length from the master
shot." "An insert differs from a cutaway as cutaways cover action not covered in the master shot." Inserts point
at details "that might be missed in a wider shot," such as "a clock, a letter, a newspaper headline, or an object
being handled"; the term usually covers objects and body parts other than the head (paraphrase).
— Wikipedia, "Insert (filmmaking)", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insert_(filmmaking) (cites Brown 2012, p. 25;
Mascelli, *The Five C's of Cinematography*).
"Inserts shots can only feature objects, and must occupy the same physical location as your scene."
— SC Lannom, StudioBinder (as 1.6). (The two sources differ on body parts.)
An insert shot before the dialogue starts should be rolled a second or two early (paraphrase). — Wikipedia,
"Camera coverage" (as 1.1).

1.14 **Today's practice: the two-shot sets up, singles carry.** Mainstream practice: "Cut a lot and move the camera
instead of moving the actors", "in the age of intensified continuity cutting"; two-shots "usually serve to set up
passages of shot/ reverse-shot cutting"; "The sustained two-shot carrying long stretches of dialogue is
increasingly rare in Hollywood cinema"; "the incessant over-the-shoulder framings of most movies".
— David Bordwell, "Where did the two-shot go? Here.", 2013-10-07,
https://www.davidbordwell.net/blog/2013/10/07/where-did-the-two-shot-go-here/
"Standard coverage of a scene will allow the viewer to keep a sense of geographical orientation."
— SC Lannom, StudioBinder (as 1.6).

## 2. Cutting — where a dialogue scene cuts

2.1 **Murch's Rule of Six**, in order of weight: emotion 51 %, story 23 %, rhythm 10 %, eye trace 7 %, the 2D plane of
the screen 5 %, 3D space 4 % (from *In the Blink of an Eye*). — Jan Sørup, "Walter Murch's Rule of Six: The
Editor's Formula for Choosing the Right Cut", filmdaft, 2025-12-02,
https://filmdaft.com/walter-murchs-rule-of-six-the-editors-formula-for-choosing-the-right-cut/ ; the six names and
emotion's 51 % "more than the other five combined" also in V Renée, "6 'Rules' for Good Cutting According to
Oscar-Winning Editor Walter Murch", No Film School, 2016-12-01,
https://nofilmschool.com/2016/11/6-rules-good-cutting-according-oscar-winning-editor-walter-murch

2.2 **Cut where the thought ends (Murch's blink).** "Start a conversation with somebody and watch when they blink";
"your listener will blink at the precise moment [the listener] 'gets' the idea"; "the cut is a 'blink' that separates
and punctuates those ideas"; "At the moment you decide to cut, what you are saying is, in effect, 'I am going to
bring this idea to an end'"; "A convincing action sequence might have around twenty-five cuts a minute".
— Will Meier, "Editing Secrets from Legendary Editor Walter Murch", Musicbed, 2019-02-15, quoting *In the Blink of
an Eye*, https://musicbed.com/blog/filmmaking/editing/editing-secrets-from-legendary-editor-walter-murch

2.3 **Dmytryk's rules of editing** (Edward Dmytryk, *On Film Editing*, as summarised by Peter Thompson), the ones that
bear on dialogue:
- "Never make a cut without a positive reason." "Stay with a shot as long as that shot is the one which best
  delivers the required information." Unnecessary close-ups weaken later close-ups (paraphrase).
- "Cut on action whenever possible." "Viewer's eyes cannot focus while moving, and moving takes 1/5 of a second."
- "Scenes should begin/end with continuing action."
- Cutting dialogue: "Exactly where, in each cut, does the editor leave the scene and exactly where does [the editor] start
  the incoming cut?"; "Listener will often respond with a grimace or reaction well before the end of the speaker's
  statement" — a sentence's meaning is clear before it ends, so the cut to the listener can come before the line
  ends (paraphrase).
- Reaction shots: cut on the reaction rather than on the words where possible (paraphrase); "Find the first frame
  of the reaction and add 3-4 frames."
- "Cut for proper values rather than proper matches."
— "Dmytryk's Rules of Editing", PDF, https://www.mariamghani.com/docs/dmytryk_eisenstein.pdf

2.4 **Show the speaker, or show the listener.** "You need to know when to show the speaker" (when the line is
poignant and reveals the speaker — paraphrase); "it can be best to show a reaction shot instead" (when one
character's line reveals something about the other — paraphrase). — Sean Berry, "Important tips to help you edit
dialogue correctly", Videomaker, https://www.videomaker.com/how-to/editing/editing-technique/important-tips-to-help-you-edit-dialogue-correctly/

2.5 **Split edits (J and L cuts).** L cut: "the audio from preceding scene overlaps the video from the following
scene"; J cut: "the audio from the following scene overlaps the video from the preceding scene". Split edits let the
audience "see context either before or after of speaking rather than simply the speaking itself"; without them "a
conversation between two people can feel like a tennis match." — Wikipedia, "Split edit",
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split_edit (cites Barry Salt, "How They Cut Dialogue Scenes"; Videomaker, "Split edit
definition and use", 2000).
J-cut: "the audio of the next scene precedes the shot change"; L-cut: "the sound or dialogue of one scene linger
into the next". Leaving or removing a pause between lines "can change the feel of a conversation" (paraphrase).
— DeGuzman, StudioBinder (as 1.5).

2.6 **Juxtaposition makes meaning (Kuleshov).** "The simple act of juxtaposing the shots in a sequence made the
relationship." — Wikipedia, "Film editing", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Film_editing (cites Arthur Knight, *The
Liveliest Art*, 1957, pp. 72–73).

2.7 **Television question-and-answer exchanges: cut to the one who replies, as the replier begins.** "As one individual finishes speaking (to
someone not in the shot), we cut to the second person just as [that person] begins to reply." (p. 47; dialogue is the main
motive for cutting between close-ups of different people in dramatic scenes and question-and-answer exchanges — paraphrase of the
sentence before.) — Edward Stasheff and Rudy Bretz (with John and Lynn Gartley), *The Television Program*, 5th ed.,
1976, PDF: https://worldradiohistory.com/BOOKSHELF-ARH/Business/The-Television-Program-5th-edition-Stasheff-et-al-1976.pdf
(read through a summarising fetch; page numbers from the running heads, may be off by one).

2.8 **Television: the reaction shot, used sparingly, cut a second early.** "It is not common, however, that the
audience is more interested in a person who is listening than in a person who is speaking." (p. 40); a student
director should not overuse reaction shots or the audience may think the camera is on the wrong person
(paraphrase); "It is wise to cut to [Speaker 1] a second early so that we may take in [Speaker 1's] expression" (p. 40). In an
question-and-answer exchange or discussion, "the reaction of the listener is often more interesting and telling than that of the
speaker" (p. 46). — Stasheff & Bretz, *The Television Program* (as 2.7).

2.9 **Television: matched reverse angles and OTS.** "Reverse-angles are most commonly used in pairs." "It is
important that they be matched, however, or they will not cut smoothly together." "use lenses at the same focal
length". OTS shots "are effective because they allow the audience to see both people, while giving the person
speaking a full-face exposure" (pp. 33–34). "As long as both cameras are on the same side of this conversation line,
no screen direction problems will arise." (p. 51). — Stasheff & Bretz, *The Television Program* (as 2.7).

## 3. Shot lengths

3.1 **Hollywood features, 1935–2010.** Average shot length in "generally linear decline across time": "about 10 s in
the 1930s and 1940s," "to below 4 s after 2000"; longest of the sample *The Seven Year Itch* (1955) 26.2 s, shortest
*Rocky IV* (1985) 2.2 s. — James E. Cutting, Kaitlin L. Brunick, Jordan E. DeLong, Catalina Iricinschi, Ayse Candan,
"Quicker, faster, darker: Changes in Hollywood film over 75 years", *i-Perception* 2(6), 2011, 569–576,
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485803/ (no figure by scene type or shot scale in it).

3.2 **Dialogue in intensified continuity: a shot per line.** "Fast cutting, down to 2 or 3 seconds per shot on
average, and a reliance on OTS's and singles typify today's intensified brand of continuity"; conversation shots are
more likely "singles rather than two-shots or ensemble framings"; "Nearly every line or reaction in the You've Got
[Mail] sequence gets a shot to itself." Measured: *The Shop Around the Corner* (1940) sidewalk scene "about 82
seconds" per shot, café scene "about 21 seconds"; *You've Got Mail* (1998) the same scenes "about 4.1 seconds" and
"4.3 seconds". Silent American films: "around five seconds per shot was common"; the arrival of sound "slowed down
the editing pace". — David Bordwell, "Intensified continuity revisited", 2007-05-27,
https://www.davidbordwell.net/blog/2007/05/27/intensified-continuity-revisited/ (read via its `/trackback/` URL).

3.3 **US multi-camera comedy series: median shot length per series**, seconds: seven late-century multi-camera
series 2.8–4.0; four earlier multi-camera series 3.2–5.3; 2000s–2010s single-camera shows 2.0–2.9. "the most common
shot type has a single speaker and a single face" (every series but one of the earliest); in multi-camera shows "two faces corresponds to two different, but both popular, shot types: the
over-the-shoulder shot and the two-shot". (The paper's results and conclusion state the trend in opposite
directions; the per-series numbers are what is quoted here.) — Taylor Arnold and Lauren Tilton, a study of pacing and production
in a collection of thirty U.S. series, *Anthology of Computers and the Humanities* vol. 3, 2025 (anthology.ach.org,
vol0003, DOI suffix 10.63744@yHo626es4FhQ)

3.4 **Television, all genres logged.** "As of September 5, 2019, the ASL of 381,698 logged shots was 6.04." Soap
operas in the log: *As the World Turns* recording parts from 3.25 s (2008) to 15.85 s (1960s); *All My Children*
(2005-12-06, part 1) 5.57 s; *Dallas* (1979–83) 5.81–8.00 s. No question-and-answer or news programme was found
in the log. — Jeremy Butler, Shot Logger (University of Alabama, 2007–2023), https://shotlogger.org/ and
https://shotlogger.org/data.php

3.5 **Hold a shot while it is the best one.** "Stay with a shot as long as that shot is the one which best delivers
the required information." — Dmytryk (as 2.3). Live television: "You don't want to cut away too soon, you don't want
to linger too long…" — Jeff Heimbuch, Chapman University Dodge
College blog (blogs.chapman.edu/dodge), 2016-09-15, quoting the director of a live-audience programme.
"There is a quick limit, luckily, to fast cutting in television" (p. 49). — Stasheff & Bretz (as 2.7).

## 4. The multi-camera case — how a production with a live audience covers a speaker

4.1 **Multi-camera, cut live by the director.** "the director creates a line cut by instructing the technical
director" "to switch between the feeds from the individual cameras"; "This is either transmitted live or recorded."
"the two outer cameras shoot close-up shots or "crosses" of the two most active characters on the set" while "the
central camera or cameras shoot a wider master shot to capture the overall action"; the method is "commonly used for
light entertainment, sports events, news, soap operas" and discussion programmes (the page's list, shortened); "the output from all cameras is recorded, both
separately and as a combined reference display" (ISO); "The multiple-camera method gives the director less control
over each shot". — Wikipedia, "Multiple-camera setup", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple-camera_setup (cites
Utterback, *Studio Television Production and Directing*, 2015; Schaefermeyer, *Digital Video BASICS*, 2012).

4.2 **A taping with an audience, 1994.** "seven cameras positioned throughout the set"; teleprompters "built into the
cameras"; the lead speaker is miked from entrance to exit; the audience is told not to stare at the cameras; a speaker
found in the crowd is moved to the front row; seven commercial breaks in the taping; "95 minutes to tape a show that
will be edited down to fit an hour-long slot"; two tapings a day, Tuesday to Thursday; an associate producer told the
audience "To get on TV, make noise" (paraphrase except the quoted parts). — Jae-Ha Kim, a behind-the-scenes report on
one taping, *Chicago Sun-Times*, 1994-12-04 (republished on jaehakim.com, 1994/12).

4.3 **The audience asks.** One speaker and one topic for an hour; the lead speaker takes the audience's questions to the
speaker on stage and moderates between them (paraphrase). — Wikipedia, the article on the programme that set this form
(no inline citation on these sentences).

4.4 **The audience's reaction is part of the programme.** "Few shows are broadcast live, they are taped in a studio
with a "cast" of nonprofessional, unrehearsed audience members." The audience's emotional reaction to what a speaker
reveals becomes part of the programme (paraphrase). — David L. Hixson, *St. James Encyclopedia of Popular Culture*
(encyclopedia.com).

4.5 **A topic, speakers on stage, a reveal.** A lead speaker brings several speakers on to discuss one emotional
topic, and a result is revealed at the end of a segment (paraphrase). — Wikipedia (cites Gamson, *Freaks Talk Back*,
1999; Kolbert, *The New York Times*, 1995-06-11). Such programmes "rely heavily on audience questions". — Ross Benes,
Mental Floss (mentalfloss.com), 2025-08-03.

4.6 **Emotion on camera.** The programme's success rests on moments when a speaker loses control on camera, and its
producers draw those moments out (paraphrase of the publisher's description, from "dozens of live tapings"). —
University of Chicago Press, description of Laura Grindstaff, *The Money Shot: Trash, Class, and the Making of TV
Talk Shows*, 2002, https://www.press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/M/bo3630693.html ; producers structuring a
programme to heighten its moment of conflict (paraphrase): Amy Agronis, UC Davis News, 2003-06-13,
https://www.ucdavis.edu/news/grindstaff-tunes-trash-tv-and-what-makes-guests-tick

4.7 **A live-audience director at work (2016).** On a speaker's entrance the crew used wide shots, then head-to-toe
shots; back from a break, the first shot zoomed in on the set rather than starting on the lead speaker (paraphrase);
"You also want to make sure you get those reaction shots, too, for bigger laughs."; when the lead speaker kept looking
at an audience member, the director cut to that person: "If we didn't, it would have been a lost opportunity, and the
people at home would have been confused." — Heimbuch, Chapman Dodge blog (as 3.5).

4.8 **The television rules for a question-and-answer exchange.** Cut to the one who replies as the replier begins
(2.7); reaction shots sparingly, a second early, the listener often more telling (2.8); matched reverse angles and OTS
on one side of the conversation line (2.9); a single-camera exchange covered by cutaways to the one asking, "noddies"
(1.12). (This line only gathers the cited rules above; it adds none.)

4.9 **The exchange is prepared, then played as spontaneous.** The “spontaneous exchanges” are, as an open secret,
somewhat planned in advance (paraphrase); "a day before the planned appearance, you have a telephone conversation with
the segment producer."; "less than half of what happened was foreseen."; a producer's cue to one speaker: "But you
should interrupt [the other speaker] and insist on telling it yourself."; of the lead speaker: "[Speaker 1] had been
completely prepared. [Speaker 1] knew the story by heart." — Roger Ebert, "The mystery of the spontaneous exchange",
2012-12-19, https://www.rogerebert.com/roger-ebert/the-mystery-of-the-spontaneous-exchange

## 5. Writing an exchange — how a story becomes lines

5.1 **A scene turns.** "A SCENE is an action through conflict in more or less continuous time and space" "that turns
the value-charged condition of a character's life on at least one value" with a degree of perceptible significance;
"No scene that doesn't turn."; every scene is rounded "by turning a value at stake in a character's life" from
positive to negative or negative to positive. — Robert McKee, *Story* (1997), quoted on McKee Seminars, "Do Your
Scenes Turn?", 2014-04-22, https://mckeestory.com/do-your-scenes-turn/
"Ideally, every scene is a story event"; each scene moves the character "closer to or farther from their ultimate
goal" (Monterusso, quoting and following McKee). — Rebecca Monterusso, "What Does It Mean to Write a Scene That
Works?", Jane Friedman, 2018-06-14, https://janefriedman.com/what-does-it-mean-to-write-a-scene-that-works/

5.2 **A beat is an action and the reaction to it.** McKee: a beat is "an exchange of behavior in action/reaction. Beat
by Beat these changing behaviors shape the turning of a scene."; "the smallest element of structure", not to be
confused with the script's "(beat)" for a short pause. — Wikipedia, "Beat (filmmaking)",
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beat_(filmmaking) (cites McKee, *Story*, 1997, pp. 35–38; Browne & King, *Self-Editing
for Fiction Writers*, 1993; Schellhardt, *Screenwriting for Dummies*, 2022, p. 218).
In a script, "(beat)" mid-dialogue means "a character is taking a second before continuing their dialogue";
"Parentheticals should always be used sparingly and with intention"; Dave Trottier: "Be judicious with
parentheticals; only use them when you must." — Joe Guerra, "What Are Parentheticals in a Script?", Backstage,
2023-09-25, https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/parentheticals-screenplay-examples-advice-76523/

5.3 **Enter late, leave early.** "enter a scene as late as possible, and exit it as early as possible" ("often
attributed to" Syd Field); "Scenes still have to make sense." — Jason Hellerman, "The Meaning of 'Start Late, Leave
Early' in Screenwriting", No Film School, 2024-01-15, https://nofilmschool.com/start-late-leave-early

5.4 **Each line in its speaker's own voice.** "Cover the Character Names": "While reading your script, cover the
character names above the dialogue." "They should have their own voice. If you find yourself confused and unsure of
which character is saying what, there's a problem." — Ken Miyamoto, "First Draft: Your Characters Lack Uniqueness?
Use These Seven Ways to Give Them Fresh Voices", MovieMaker, 2017-11-03,
https://www.moviemaker.com/first-draft-seven-ways-to-give-your-characters-fresh-voices/3
"Each character should have a well-defined voice" (from background and beliefs, not only accent — paraphrase).
— Joe Guerra, "Talk the Talk: How to Write Great Dialogue", Backstage, 2024-06-25,
https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/how-to-write-good-dialogue-77417/
"Every word of dialogue must be true to your character."; "To make your characters' dialogue more identifiable
consider using contractions, colloquialisms, slang."; "Writing character biographies in the first person (in their
voices) for all of your characters". — Susan Kouguell, "Top 10 Tips on Writing Dynamic Dialogue", Script Magazine,
2020-06-12, https://scriptmag.com/screenplays/top-10-tips-on-writing-dynamic-dialogue

5.5 **Not on the nose; no explaining.** On-the-nose dialogue "states exactly what a character thinks, feels, wants,
or means in a direct and obvious way"; the test: "does this feel like something the character would actually say in
this moment"; "Good dialogue gives the actor a playable action. Weak dialogue gives them a statement."; "If a line is
obvious for a reason, that is a choice." — HowToFilmSchool, "On-the-Nose Dialogue" (no author; modified 2026-03-23),
https://howtofilmschool.com/dictionary/on-the-nose-dialogue/
"People don't always say exactly what's on their mind." — Kouguell (as 5.4).
"characters should rarely be speaking their feelings out loud at all"; "A cardinal sin of dialogue writing is just
having characters explain background or context information."; "Great dialogue is generally short and sweet."
— Guerra, Backstage 2024 (as 5.4).

5.6 **Listening, answering, pausing, interrupting.** "Keep in mind how your characters listen or don't listen to each
other."; "Consider silences and pauses your characters might use."; "Dialogue must not sound wooden or stilted."
— Kouguell (as 5.4). "People don't just talk *at* each other; they respond"; "Study the use of repetition,
interruption, and back-and-forth" (Mamet, *Glengarry Glen Ross*). — Guerra, Backstage 2024 (as 5.4).

5.7 **How an interruption is written.** "double-dashes are usually the safer bet for interruption, as they indicate an
abrupt cutoff"; "an ellipsis implies the idea trailed off"; both "indicate an unfinished thought"; an "interrupting"
parenthetical may be added if needed (paraphrase). — John August, "Interrupted Dialogue: How to Format Character
Interruptions", Quote-Unquote Apps blog, 2025-12-30,
https://blog.quoteunquoteapps.com/interrupted-dialogue-how-to-format-character-interruptions/

5.8 **Conversation's own order (conversation analysis).** Speakers "speak one at a time in alternating turns"; a turn
may pass at the end of a turn-construction unit, "a point where the turn may end and a new speaker may begin"; at that
point, in order: the current speaker selects the next; else the first to speak takes the turn; else the current
speaker goes on (paraphrase). "the majority of overlaps are non-problematic" (Schegloff 2000); "power" interruptions
are hostile and change the topic, "rapport" interruptions cooperate (Goldberg 1990, paraphrase). — Wikipedia,
"Turn-taking", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn-taking (cites Sacks, Schegloff & Jefferson, "A Simplest
Systematics for the Organization of Turn-Taking for Conversation", *Language* 50(4), 1974).
"An adjacency pair is composed of two utterances by two speakers, one after the other"; "question → answer" is one;

"Ask open-ended questions when possible."; "Double-barreled questions (asking more than one question at a time)

Your Journalism Better*, NewsU), https://www.poynter.org/news/interview-let-your-questions-guide-conversation
Open-ended questions "Encourage detailed responses"; closed ones are "Useful for clarifying facts"; follow-ups "Show

### 7.1 Coverage and staging (Katz)

Katz page numbers: PDF page = printed + 13. Katz calls actors "players" and the arrangement of a scene "staging".

7.1.1 **Shot sizes are relative, and named by framing height.** The long, medium and close shot "are overlapping
portions of a single space and only make sense in relation to one another" (p. 121). Figure 6.1, "Basic Framing
heights for the human figure" (p. 122), names nine framings, tightest first: extreme close-up, medium close-up, full
close-up, wide close-up, close shot, medium close shot, medium shot, medium full shot, full shot (read from the
drawing, each line is the frame's lower edge: just below the chin, the neck, the shoulders, the upper chest, the
mid-chest, the belt, below the hips, below the knees, below the feet — paraphrase of the figure). "The move from wide shot to close-up was
considered too radical a jump for audiences during the first five decades of motion pictures unless a medium shot was
used in between." "Today … audiences easily accept extreme changes in scale." (p. 121).

7.1.2 **Which size carries dialogue.** "For dialogue sequences the shoulder-and-head shot has become the predominant
framing" (television's influence, p. 123). The medium shot "was the workhorse for dialogue scenes throughout the
sound period" and "captures an actor's gestures and body language, but is still tight enough to include subtle
variations in facial expression"; group shots for dialogue are composed in its range — "The two-shot, three-shot,
four-shot or five-shot are the typical groupings"; above five players "the camera often must pull back into the
full-shot range" (pp. 127-128). Today the medium shot is used "in conjunction with close-ups—not as the primary setup
for a scene" (p. 128). The full shot is "relegated to the function of an establishing shot when it is necessary to
connect a character and a location in a single shot"; "If the long shot is used with these two tighter framings, the
editing pattern invariably moves in close and does not return to the full shot."; "a close-up generally must be
accompanied by other close-ups, medium or full shots to fulfill the narrative requirements of a scene." (pp. 128-129).
Framing a single: "leave extra space on the side of the screen the subject is looking at and more space at the bottom
of the frame than at the top" (pp. 124-125); off-centre alternate close-ups create "a left/right eye motion that is
dynamic" — "compositions are not judged individually but by how they combine in a sequence" (p. 124).

7.1.3 **The line of action and the triangle of setups.** The line "is usually the line of sight between subjects
featured in a scene"; "only camera positions within the established semicircle are permitted"; cutting shots from both
sides: "the man is looking at the back of the woman's head" (p. 130). The triangle system: "all the basic shots
possible for any subject can be taken from three points within the 180-degree working space"; "Any shot can be joined
to any other shot in the triangle system of setups"; "It is used extensively for live television programs such as quiz
shows, sports programs and sit-coms." (pp. 130-131). "There are five basic camera setups that can be obtained within
the triangle: Angular singles (medium shots or close-ups), master two-shots, over-the-shoulder shots, point-of-view
singles (medium shots or close-ups) and profile shots." (p. 132); the middle camera "always obtains the two-shot"
(p. 133).

7.1.4 **A new line: the pivot shot, and reusing setups.** "The establishment of a new line is usually set up with a
shot of a person who turns [the person's] attention to a new area or person within the frame. This pivot shot joins the two lines
of action." A return to a player seen on the old line is "a reestablishing shot". "Conventional wisdom advocates
reusing lines of action and the corresponding camera setups so that a consistent sense of space is reinforced through
repetition." (p. 135). A player who crosses the line must be "seen clearly in a shot that permits the viewer to
reorient [oneself]" (p. 136); an uninterrupted camera move may cross (p. 138); a cutaway is "a quick fix in the editing
process" (pp. 138-139). "As a rule, the working area chosen for each new line of action keeps the camera in the center
of the group when shooting dialogue situations at a table or in a confined space." (p. 138). Katz's own view: of the
180-degree rule, "many of its assumptions are overstated" (p. 142); "Do not automatically rule out a shot simply
because it means crossing the line. If it works, use it." (p. 187).

7.1.5 **Two players (ch. 9).** The master shot "is the one shot that is wide enough to include all the actors in the
scene and that runs for the entire length of the action"; usually "part of a plan of coverage that includes other
camera setups in the triangle system", but sometimes "the only shot the director feels is necessary" (p. 174); the
master is the view in which "the entire dramatic action could be understood if no other shots were used (as opposed to
the wider establishing shot)" (glossary, p. 360). The medium two-shot, "plan Americain", covered whole dialogue
sequences in the early 1930s; "Entire scenes can be staged effectively at this distance without ever resorting to a
close-up." (p. 175). Shot / reverse shot: "No cutting strategy better represents the Hollywood style than this one";
its two advantages over the two-shot: "we get to see a subject's isolated reaction to dialogue; the second is that the
point of view changes within the scene", and the eye-line match gives "a sense of spatial unity" (p. 175). Sight lines:
"the closer the sight line is to the camera the more intimate our contact with the actor will be"; a look into the
lens is "very confrontational"; usually sight lines fall "slightly to the left or right of the camera", and "it is
common practice to maintain the same distance from the camera for sight lines in alternating close-ups of two or more
actors" (pp. 175-176). OTS: "usually shot as matched pairs maintaining consistent lens and framing choices … In a
continuous exchange of dialogue, however, matched pairs are more commonplace." (p. 179). Low-angle "over-the-hip"
reverses "tend to put the subjects in an adversarial relationship" (p. 181). Close-ups whose subject looks toward the
camera "give a stronger sense of presence" (p. 183). Stagings without eye contact "create tension" (p. 185); a high
angle "usually serves to create tension and isolation" (p. 186). "As a general rule the player whose eyes are most
clearly seen will dominate the shot"; "in-depth staging encourages our indentification with the foreground player"
(p. 190; also p. 188). Of one staging: "overcutting destroys the unity of the scene" (p. 188). Katz's system: three
patterns of players seen from above, "A", "I" and "L"; "The only arrangement for two subjects is the I pattern"
(p. 176); "The I pattern for two players is the basic building block in our system. This is because the line of action
can be established between only two people at a time." (p. 177); ten positions of the I pattern follow (pp. 178-193).

7.1.6 **Three players (ch. 10).** A third player framed between the other two is the A pattern, lined up outside them
the L pattern (p. 195). "the scene should have a basic viewing orientation"; "the general idea is to reuse positions
when possible rather than constantly creating new ones whenever a new line is established" (p. 196, Fig. 10.3).
Singles after the master "tends to fragment space, and secondly, it prevents us from seeing the player speaking and
the players who are listening in one shot"; instead "OTS shots have been used to unify space. After the establishing
shot, an entire conversation can be comfortably handled in OTS shots and an occasional three-shot or two-shot to vary
the rhythm." (pp. 197-198). The A pattern "is a direct positioning of opposed figures as might be expected in an
interview or meeting in which the parties are on a formal basis" (p. 198): the man whose sight line is near the camera
reads as "in a position of authority", the women in profile as "the listeners"; a down shot of them "is not the type of
framing we would expect if either woman had anything important to say"; out of the frontal position the man is "in a
reactive position" (pp. 198-199). Overlapping space "ties together the scene space"; shots of the man turning [the man's] head
serve as pivot shots to each woman's close-up: "The direction of the man's look is the determining factor. Cutting on
[the man's] head turn is a powerful editing cue." (p. 200). "Almost always, the L seating or standing arrangement occurs when a
single player is addressing two others", the two grouped in a two-shot (p. 202). Opening on a two-shot and showing the
whole space only later is "deliberately withholding the full context of the scene" (p. 202). A wide establishing shot
late "might serve as a dramatic pause in the middle of the scene or as the final shot" (p. 205). "Throughout this
sequence cutting has been motivated by either action or the cut on the look." (p. 207).

7.1.7 **Four or more players (ch. 11).** Five players give "approximately 27 shots"; group staging "is a matter of
consolidation and simplification" (p. 209). "Even when one person gains the attention of the other guests, it's
basically a two-person arrangement: the speaker and [the speaker's] audience. When filming a large group where several players
speak, close-ups are often used in favor of three- and four-player group shots, since this helps differentiate the
players." (p. 209). "The key to camera positioning is identifying which players are the central players in the
scene"; the line is set between "the principal two players" (p. 210). "Limit yourself to a few key setups. If you are
establishing more than four sightlines to move around space in a large group, you are creating unnecessary problems."
— a "general viewing direction" (p. 211). Five at a round table: the main angle over one player's shoulder sees all the
others; "Only one reverse is required to see the grandmother." (p. 212). "In midsized groups where each subject is
dramatically significant, master shot stagings are often desirable." (p. 215). In a crowd the camera is inside with a
wide or normal lens or outside with a telephoto; "dialogue stagings can be shot with a telephoto" (pp. 217-218).

7.1.8 **Action and reaction; inserts; point of view.** "there are only two types of shots in a conversation, the
action and the reaction. Filmmakers are frequently overconcerned about the action. The truth is, we learn as much from
the reaction as we do from the action." — "the person speaking need not be the center of attention"; the reaction
"doesn't have to be a CU of the man's face. It could also be a CU of [the man's] hands" (pp. 227-228). "the notion that an
important line of dialogue requires a close-up is simply not true." (p. 228). Glossary: REACTION SHOT "In a dialogue
scene the shot of a player listening while another player's voice continues on the sound track. Most reaction shots
are close-ups." (p. 361); INSERT "Usually a close-up showing an important detail of a scene." (p. 359); CUTAWAY "A single
shot inserted into a sequence that momentarily interrupts the general flow of action" (p. 358); ESTABLISHING SHOT
"Typically a long shot at the beginning of a scene designed to inform viewers of a change in location and to orient
them to the general mood and relative placement of subjects in the scene." (p. 358). Point of view (ch. 16): "the closer
the sight line of a player in CU is to the camera, the greater the degree of viewer identification" (p. 269);
"Identification usually will side with the subject given the most screen time." (p. 274); yet "the type of shot and the
cooperation between shots is more important in developing a consistent point of view than the number of shots given
to a particular character", and two shots of one player "from the same angle build identification more strongly than
the three shots of the man, each from a different angle" (p. 275).

### 7.2 Cutting (Katz, Murch)

7.2.1 **The cut on the look (Katz p. 123 and after).** "Jean-Luc Godard once said that the most natural cut is the cut
on the look." "A look can tell us that an object out of frame is of interest, and it can tell us in which direction
the object is located." "the eyeline of a subject clearly determines spatial relations in the scene space. Viewers are
particularly sensitive to incongruities in the sight lines between subjects who are looking at each other and in most
situations can easily detect when the eye match is slightly off." (p. 123). "In editing, the most powerful cuing device
is the sight line of an actor in CU." (p. 268). The simplest question-and-answer pattern is "a shot of a person looking
offscreen followed by a shot of the object the person is observing" (p. 147); the cut can be reversed, the object seen
"before the look" (p. 150; also p. 270). A head turned to listen to something offscreen is "an aural version of the cut
on the look" (p. 192; also p. 271). A cut on the look need not be a point-of-view shot: "This is a cut on the look, but
it is not a POV shot. That would require a reverse angle." (p. 205). A dolly in to a moment of realization "is often set
up as a cut on the look" (p. 298). (Katz gives no timing for the cut on the look.)

7.2.2 **Editing is structure: what the viewer knows and when (Katz ch. 7).** Each shot "contains narrative and graphic
information that predetermines key editing decisions such as the length and order of shots"; "Structure controls the
order in which the story information is given to the viewer." (p. 145). Continuity editing rests on question and
answer: temporal, spatial and logical connections (p. 146); "Nearly all editing strategies in narrative film are devised
to set up a framework of expectations in a series of shots. The result is narrative motion." (p. 147). "the main
concern for the visualizer is not the pictorial elements of a shot or sequence, but the structure of the sequence—or, to
put it another way, what the viewer knows and when." (p. 148). An answer shown before its question creates suspense
(p. 148). The classic establishing shot "merely shows us what we already expect and does little to raise expectations";
the alternative is separate detail shots that the viewer connects; "storytelling should engage the viewer at every
point" (pp. 151-152).

7.2.3 **Camera cutting and coverage (Katz pp. 152-153).** Shooting a full storyboard as drawn is "cutting in the
camera", where "even the lengths of shots can be estimated"; coverage "employs several camera setups for every action,
ensuring that a logical sequence can be cut together"; "A formulaic selection of wide, medium and close-up shots is
usually sufficient for a basic rendition of any scene"; "While coverage is an extremely safe way to shoot, it is also
uninspired" (p. 152). A father speaking to the children, covered: a medium shot and close-up of the father, the same of
the kids, a three-shot OTS of the kids and a reverse OTS of the father — "a total of eight setups" (p. 153). In coverage
"action is played out in full for most setups … This is most useful when filming dialogue." (p. 153).

7.2.4 **Cutting on movement, exits and entrances (Katz pp. 154-156).** "the common practice in the continuity style
would locate the cut somewhere into the action rather than before or after"; "Cutting on the action is found in
virtually all types of sequences whether the subject of the shot is lifting a drink to [the subject's] lips or merely turning
[the subject's] head or moving [the subject's] eyes."; directors "stage action so that it will overlap an anticipated edit point" (pp. 154-155).
An exit or entrance is cut "while the subject is still partially within the frame" (p. 155). Clearing the frame: hold the
empty frame "for a minimum of 1-2 seconds" (p. 155); opening the next shot with the subject already in frame "is not a
common practice in the continuity style since it is somewhat abrupt" (p. 155). "the filmmaker will visualize how long
certain actions should be viewed before moving to another shot. [The filmmaker] will then try to plan action at that point so that
editing is motivated visually." (p. 156). Indirection: "Not every story point and line of dialogue in a scene needs to
be emphasized." (p. 228).

Murch: the ebook carries no page numbers on its pages; it is cited by chapter (the contents list gives the chapters'
print pages: "The Rule of Six" 17, "Dragnet" 64, "A Galaxy of Winking Dots" 70; the blink passage is in "Don't Worry,
It's Only a Movie", 57).

7.2.5 **The Rule of Six (Murch, "The Rule of Six").** "An ideal cut (for me) is the one that satisfies all the following
six criteria at once: 1) it is true to the emotion of the moment; 2) it advances the story; 3) it occurs at a moment that
is rhythmically interesting and "right"; 4) it acknowledges what you might call "eye-trace"—the concern with the location
and movement of the audience's focus of interest within the frame; 5) it respects "planarity"—the grammar of three
dimensions transposed by photography to two (the questions of stage-line, etc.); 6) and it respects the three-dimensional
continuity of the actual space (where people are in the room and in relation to one another)." "Emotion, at the top of
the list, is the thing that you should try to preserve at all costs. If you find you have to sacrifice certain of those
six things to make a cut, sacrifice your way up, item by item, from the bottom." "the top two on the list (emotion and
story) are worth far more than the bottom four … under most circumstances, the top of the list—emotion—is worth more than
all five of the things underneath it." "satisfying the criteria of items higher on the list tends to obscure problems
with items lower on the list, but not vice-versa"; "those top three things on the list—emotion, story, rhythm—are
extremely tightly connected"; "never accept less when more is available to you"; "don't ever give up emotion before
story. Don't give up story before rhythm, don't give up rhythm before eye-trace, don't give up eye-trace before planarity,
and don't give up planarity before spatial continuity". "What they finally remember is not the editing, not the
camerawork, not the performances, not even the story—it's how they felt." Of a multi-camera comedy series, where the actors are
always spatially "correct": that "absolutely does not prevent bad cuts from being made all the time" (note 9). (The text
speaks of "The values I put after each item", but the values themselves are in neither the text layer nor any image of
this PDF; see §6.)

7.2.6 **Do the most with the least; where not to cut (Murch, "Most with the Least", "Why Do Cuts Work?").** "attempt to
produce the greatest effect in the viewer's mind by the least number of things on screen" — "suggestion is always more
effective than exposition"; "it takes more work and discernment to decide where not to cut"; "An overactive editor, who
changes shots too frequently, is like a tour guide who can't stop pointing things out". Discontinuity "allows us to
choose the best camera angle for each emotion and story point". A jump that is "neither subtle nor total" — "Cutting from
a full-figure master shot, for instance, to a slightly tighter shot that frames the actors from the ankles up" — produces
"a mental jarring—a jump— that is comparatively disturbing" ("Why Do Cuts Work?"). The editor's preoccupation: "What is
the audience going to be thinking at any particular moment? Where are they going to be looking?" ("Misdirection").

7.2.7 **The blink: cut where the listener "gets" the idea (Murch, "Don't Worry, It's Only a Movie").** "your listener will
blink at the precise moment [the listener] "gets" the idea of what you are saying, not an instant earlier or later"; the blink
comes when the listener realizes the "introduction" is over or feels the speaker is "winding down"; "And that blink will
occur where a cut could have happened, had the conversation been filmed. Not a frame earlier or later." "a shot presents
us with an idea, or a sequence of ideas, and the cut is a "blink" that separates and punctuates those ideas. At the moment
you decide to cut, what you are saying is, in effect, "I am going to bring this idea to an end and start something
new."" "the cut by itself does not create the "blink moment"—the tail does not wag the dog." The rate of blinking is
"geared more to our emotional state and to the nature and frequency of our thoughts".

7.2.8 **Speaker and listener: the branch points (Murch, "Dragnet").** A good actor's blinks fall where the character's
would; Murch marks a shot's "out point" "in real time", never "by inching back and forth". The "Dragnet" system: "keep
every word of dialogue on screen. When someone had finished speaking, there was a brief pause and then a cut to the
person, who was now about to talk" — "a shallow simplicity that doesn't reflect the grammar of complex exchanges". "while
that person is still talking, you will turn to look at the listener to find out what [the listener] thinks of what is being said. The
question is, "When exactly do you turn?"" "Every shot has potential "cut points" the way a tree has branches". "by
cutting away from a certain character before [the character] finishes speaking, I might encourage the audience to think only about the
face value of what [the character] said. On the other hand, if I linger on the character after [the character] finishes speaking, I allow the
audience to see, from the expression in [the character's] eyes, that [the character] is probably not telling the truth" — "Either I cut away while [the character]
is speaking (branch number one) or I hold until the audience realizes [the character] is lying (branch number two), but I cannot cut in
between those two branches". "Where you feel comfortable blinking—if you are really listening to what is being said—is
where the cut will feel right." The three problems: "1) identifying a series of potential cut points … 2) determining
what effect each cut point will have on the audience, and 3) choosing which of those effects is the correct one for the
film." Rates: real-world blinking "somewhere between the extremes of four and forty blinks per minute"; "a convincing
action sequence might have around twenty-five cuts a minute, whereas a dialogue scene would still feel "normal" (in an
American film) averaging six cuts per minute or less." (six cuts a minute is an average shot of ten seconds or more —
arithmetic, not Murch's words). "You should be right with the blinks, perhaps leading them ever so slightly." To watch a
fight "as a phenomenon in itself" — "you would reduce the number of cuts considerably" (note 11).

### 7.3 The multi-camera production case (Grindstaff)

Grindstaff interned on two nationally televised live-audience programmes the book calls *Show 1* and *Show 2* (pseudonyms, p. 2
n. 1; p. 8) and attended live tapings of more than two dozen different programmes of the form (p. 65, paraphrase).

7.3.1 **The "money shot", defined.** Ordinary speakers are "expected to deliver what I call, borrowing from film
pornography, the "money shot" of the [the form's] text: joy, sorrow, rage, or remorse expressed in visible, bodily
terms. It is the moment when tears well up in a woman's eyes and [Speaker 2's] voice catches in sadness and pain as [Speaker 2]
describes having lost [Speaker 2's] child to a preventable disease; when a man tells [the partner] that [the speaker has] been sleeping
with another woman and [the partner's] jaw drops in rage and disbelief; when members of the … audience lose their composure
as they listen to a victim recount the lurid details of a crime." (pp. 19-20). "the money shot: concrete, physical
evidence of real, raw emotion." (p. 116). "the talk itself—is often the least important part of any given show. What
matters is bodily evidence of a guest's emotional investment" … "virtually all production efforts— choosing topics;
finding, interviewing, and rehearsing guests; coaching audience members—are geared toward maximizing the
probability of its display." (p. 21). Of a topic, producers ask "is it visual? … by visual, producers mean
"emotional" or "volatile."" (p. 84). "All good television is built around moments of dramatic revelation." … "hence
the importance of slow motion and the close-up." (p. 250).

7.3.2 **What brings it on.** "Producers have an easier time leading guests to the emotional brink if the topic is
sensitive or volatile to begin with, if the guest feels strongly about it, if the guest is being surprised, or if
producers have found others with an opposing viewpoint to challenge [Speaker 2] on the air." (p. 116). "You can be
guaranteed fireworks when you have two people who disagree and both feel 100 percent right about their stance."
(p. 103; again p. 125). Planting people with a stake in the audience locates "the potential for dramatic
interaction in the strategic juxtaposition of participants" (p. 122). Of a confrontation: "you tell [Speaker 1] to
mention [Speaker 2] in the third segment—you know what I mean? It doesn't take a brain surgeon to figure out the rest."
(pp. 125-126).

7.3.3 **The order of speakers: the strongest goes first.** "In the actual staging of a show, producers follow the same
pyramid structure that they urge on audience members and guests. The most important guest always goes on first,
setting the tone and framework for subsequent events (in a conflict situation, this person is typically the heroine
of the story, the person who feels victimized by a past wrong or injustice)." "Important" "often means that [Speaker 2] is
the most energetic and the best talker and thus the one most likely to hold viewers' interest and prevent them from
changing the channel." (p. 123). "'Lead with your most explosive story.'" (a *Show 2* producer, p. 124). Speakers are
re-ordered at the last minute after the green room ("Dammit, start with [that speaker]!", p. 123), a segment may run "longer than
planned" (p. 124), and a programme can be re-ordered "after the fact through careful editing" (p. 124); "'Let this go
long, dump that, move that!'" (p. 124 n. 2). One *Show 1* programme led with the more emotional speaker "to draw the audience
in" and brought the other on "later as a success story" (p. 5). A drunk-driving programme's order: a crash survivor first
("an easy cry" with "fantastic visuals"), then a father and son, then a MADD representative, then a local politician;
the father and son broke down onstage — "We ended up doing two segments on them" — which the producer put down to
"timing and their placement in the lineup" (pp. 102-103). Another opened with each panelist's testimony, which "set
the tone for the following segments, which included testimony from the panelists' family members and friends."
(p. 103 n. 5). Experts come "only during the last segment of a show to mediate between hostile parties or lend an air
of professional legitimacy", sometimes seated in the audience (p. 89); producers call that segment "Siberia" (p. 221).

7.3.4 **Speakers brought on segment by segment.** Most of the programmes "tended to stack the panel with multiple stories, each
with a different set of guests" (p. 8). One *Show 2* taping, in order (pp. 12-13): the programme "began with [Lead 2], the host,
sitting on the bench between [Speaker 3] and [Speaker 4]"; "[Speaker 5] took the stage at the beginning of the second segment" (the
producer to [Speaker 5] in the wings: "this is where the intervention begins"); "in the third segment, the producers
reintroduced [Speaker 4]"; Speaker 1 "announced to the audience that there was another family member waiting to speak with
[Speaker 3]: [Speaker 3's] older sister, [Speaker 6]. [Speaker 6] then rounded the corner and met [Speaker 3's] gaze"; "Toward the end of the segment,
the arguing between [Speaker 5] and [Speaker 4] escalated, and the audience, emboldened, taunted [Speaker 4]"; "Just before the show
ended, when everyone was yelling at everyone else", Speaker 1 made an offer that "brought the audience to its feet".
Holding a fact back for a later segment: a late speaker "has to be moved to a later segment, and that means that Guests
3 and 4 can't mention [the late speaker's] affair until after [the late speaker's] wife joins [the late speaker] onstage." (p. 117). A speaker surprised "during the third
segment, out came "the opposition"" (p. 182). Before a break Speaker 1 reads a tease ("Up next is a woman whose … Don't
go away!") — "This was [Speaker 7's] cue to walk onstage." (p. 5); a lead speaker "turned to the camera and said, "Don't go away,
we'll be right back," thus signaling the transition to a commercial break" just as Speaker 2 "was about to relay the
details of [Speaker 2's] dramatic escape" (p. 46). (The book's one use of "escalated" is the one above, inside a segment, p. 13;
it states no rule that segments escalate. The ordering rule it states is 7.3.3.)

7.3.5 **How a speaker's answer is shaped.** Producers want speakers "to tell their stories in sound bites, following the
conventional pyramid structure employed by journalists" (p. 100); "They have to say stuff in the right order, and in a
nutshell, what's going on, so viewers will be interested." (p. 100); "start with the highlight of the answer and, once
they hit that, they can digress all they want" … "Say what you found and then how you got there. Don't build up to a
point." (pp. 100-101); "you've got to tell [Lead 2] and the audience first what is going on now, why you're even on this
show. You can't wait until the last segment" (p. 120 n. 1). Producers fear speakers will "not disclose the most important
information first" or "go into too much detail", yet "it is better to have a guest wander and digress than give simple
yes or no answers" (p. 116). Not word for word: "It's not a good thing to rehearse what you're going to say word for
word because, when you get out there, you will sound distant" (p. 100). Coached lines, said in Speaker 2's own way: a
producer gives a speaker a line ("Say, 'Girlfriend, we're sisters …'"); Speaker 2 answers "I don't say girlfriend; I don't talk
like that"; the producer: "Of course, say it in your own way" … "Don't be afraid to butt in, interrupt, do whatever it takes to
get your point across— don't wait for the host to call on you" (p. 12; the same coaching p. 19). The producers
"prepared them for key questions that [Lead 1], the host, would ask on the air" (p. 3); the research "informs the
questions that producers prepare for the host" (p. 99). Between segments producers "run over to guests from the
sidelines to whisper a few hurried words" (p. 120).

7.3.6 **The audience and its reaction shots.** The audience in the studio is "an integral part of the genre" (p. 121). The
warm-up is "to raise the energy level in the room" (p. 122); "They practice oohing, aahing, and clapping furiously.
During the taping itself, the warm-up person will often lead these reactions from the sidelines" (p. 123); etiquette
"forbids … mugging for the camera, or speaking without being called on but encourages "big reactions" to the events
onstage." (p. 66). "the bigger the reaction and the more expressive the behavior, the more likely you are to attract
the camera"; sitting "near the front of the stage" "increases the chance of getting targeted for a reaction shot",
on the aisle "the chance of getting chosen by the host to ask a question" (p. 127); "the cameras will avoid those
sections of the audience in which anyone waves, makes faces, or shouts out unsolicited comments." (pp. 127-128). After
the taping, audience members "remain seated, either to provide additional reaction shots for the camera (inserted at
appropriate moments later in the editing process) or to serve as a backdrop for the host as [Speaker 1] does "promos""
(p. 129). Audience questions follow "the same pyramid structure" as speakers' answers; the warm-up comedian "sometimes
screens potential questions during the breaks between segments" (p. 122); the questions are "predictable and
formulaic" and "function much the same way a Greek chorus does" (p. 128). On the louder shows audiences "provide
running commentary (groaning, gasping, hissing, booing) to events onstage and clap wildly going in and out of
commercial breaks"; "each time a new guest was ambushed with surprising information, the audience cheered" (p. 67).
In the *Show 1* prologue, audience members "began to denounce [Speaker 2]"; "A short, gray-haired, elderly woman stood up and
called [Speaker 2] a messenger of the devil" (p. 5).

7.3.7 **The control room during a taping.** The booth: "cool and dark, illuminated primarily by the double row of
television monitors" (p. 4). "'Cold open—no music, no applause!' the director shouted. 'Three! Two! One! Roll tape!'
The camera was tight on the first guest, [Speaker 8]" … "[Lead 1], the show's host, prodded for more details, and [Speaker 8]
obliged" … "The segment ended with the introductory credit sequence accompanied by the trademark [Show 1] music, and then
the director cut to a commercial." (p. 4). Speaker 1 "read the following tease off the prompter before breaking to
another commercial" (p. 5). Speakers not yet on watch from the control room, a producer there to "keep them pumped up"
and "help them identify points to respond to when it was their turn to go out onstage" (p. 12); a producer tells a
waiting speaker that Speaker 2 "must talk directly to [Speaker 3] and get to the point. They were running out of time" (p. 13).
Photographs and old footage are used "to vary the pace and keep audience members engaged" (pp. 6-7); on-screen facts
may be "inserted later during the final edit, either along with the credits or as transitions in and out of
commercial breaks" (p. 11). "actual air time is only forty-two minutes, tapings can take several hours" (p. 129).
Behind-the-scenes footage (speakers walking on and off, the green room afterwards) is used "either as a coda to the
episode or as brief "teasers"" (p. 124).

7.3.8 **What Grindstaff does not give.** No camera plan: how many cameras, which holds whom, when the director cuts
between speaker, lead speaker and audience. The only camera lines are in 7.3.6-7.3.7 (p. 4 "tight on the first guest";
pp. 127-129 reaction shots). (The library README gives "pp. 128, 130" for the reaction shots taped after: the sentence
is on p. 129; p. 130 carries a photo caption, Figure 8.)

### 7.4 Storyboards (Katz ch. 3)

7.4.1 **What a storyboard is for.** "Storyboards serve two purposes: First, they allow a filmmaker to previsualize [the filmmaker's]
ideas and refine them in the same way a writer develops ideas through successive drafts; secondly, they serve as the
clearest language to communicate ideas to the entire production team." They "are not restricted to action scenes"; "Even
small, dramatic films can benefit from storyboards, helping the director to refine mood and dialogue." (p. 24). "For
directors without a strong visual sense the storyboard illustrator is the shot-flow designer, essential to the
structuring, staging and composition of shots and sequences." (p. 23). "storyboards are helpful to the director whether
[the director] follows them during shooting or not. For instance, after large portions of the script are boarded the director is
able to see the dramatic flow of the story in a way that the screenplay fails to reveal." (p. 50).

7.4.2 **What a panel carries.** "Storyboards basically convey two kinds of information: a description of the physical
environment of the sequence (set design/location) and a description of the spatial quality of a sequence (staging,
camera angle, lens and the movement of any elements in the shot)." (pp. 44-45). "Above all, the storyboard conveys the
shot flow of the scene, which is the combination of dramatic and graphic design … practically speaking, the use of
viewpoint, lens perspective and narrative motion are paramount." (pp. 64-65). On a *Citizen Kane* board, "Descriptions
below each panel describe the basic action of the scene, transitions and camera movement." (p. 28). Hitchcock's
*Lifeboat* page "includes notes describing action and the lines of dialogue that accompany the panels. The specific
dialogue cues mean that Hitchcock was cutting in the camera" (p. 37).

7.4.3 **What a panel cannot show, and how it is written instead.** "The most obvious limitation of the storyboard is
its inability to show motion—not merely action within the frame, but more importantly, the movement of the camera";
"The most obvious solution is to use captions and schematic drawings to describe what cannot be drawn." (p. 39). A
frame within a frame with connecting arrows marks a dolly or zoom; without the connecting lines it means "a change in
shot size is accomplished with a cut to a new shot" (p. 42, Fig. 3.4); "There is no standardization in any of this
iconography" (p. 40); eleven panels can stand for "a single, unbroken shot" (p. 42). The simplest board is "written
description and arrows"; "a director with editorial experience will be able to read the board and get a sense of the
pacing" (p. 46). Aerial schematics "describe camera placement exactly" but "give little indication of shot size or the
emotional or kinetic quality of a shot"; stick figures do not show "the height of the camera" (p. 47). The speed of a
camera move and "the transfer of attention from one actor to another … cannot be anticipated in a storyboard" (p. 227).

7.4.4 **Reading and revising boards.** A flip-book presentation lets one "vary the speed at which [the reader] turns the pages …
to simulate editing rhythms", and panels "can be easily added, removed or reordered in much the same way an editor
makes changes in the actual film" (pp. 43-44). "Reading a storyboard is a matter of editing with your eyes." (p. 189).
"It's what you leave out that counts." (p. 51). Quick thumbnails take "1-3 minutes" a frame, so "an entire sequence can
be talked out and drawn within an hour" (p. 52). "all storyboards are adaptations, since they are transposed from a
screenplay"; the board "may come very close to being another draft of the screenplay"; illustrators "restructure scenes,
add story elements and contribute dialogue" (pp. 66, 72). In *Empire of the Sun* as filmed, "By omitting the in-between
shots of [Character 1] picking the flashlight up and going to the window, the scene is streamlined and the audience is surprised
(but not confused)" (p. 81).

### 7.5 Writing the exchange (McKee)

Robert McKee, *Dialogue: The Art of Verbal Action for Page, Stage, and Screen*, Twelve, 2016 ("McKee"). The PDF is an
ebook without page numbers; it is cited by chapter.

7.5.1 **A line is an action on the other (ch. 1, "The Full Definition of Dialogue").** "all speech is an outward
execution of an inner action. All talk responds to a need, engages a purpose, and performs an action. … no character ever
talks to anyone, even to [oneself], for no reason, to do nothing. Therefore, beneath every line of character talk, the
writer must create a desire, intent, and action." "To say something is to do something"; each line "moves the scene [the character is]
in from one beat to the next, while at the same time, it dynamically propels [the character] closer to (positive) or further from
(negative) the satisfaction of [the character's] core desire. Dialogue-as-action is the foundation principle of this book."
Dramatized dialogue "sends lines back and forth between characters in conflict. Each line contains an action with a
specific intention and causes a reaction somewhere within the scene." By medium: "screenplays favor image over voice";
"Teleplays tend to balance voice and image". Ch. 2 ("The Three Functions of Dialogue"): the functions are "exposition,
characterization, action"; "Dialogue is what characters do to each other" (quoting Elizabeth Bowen); "before writing a
line, ask these questions: What does my character want out of this situation? At this precise moment, what action would
[the character] take in an effort to reach that desire? What exact words would [the character] use to carry out that action?"; name the action
under a line "with an active gerund ("-ing") phrase".

7.5.2 **The six tasks (Part Two, introduction, "Six Dialogue Tasks").** "Effective dialogue executes six tasks
simultaneously: 1. Each verbal expression takes an inner action. 2. Each beat of action/reaction intensifies the scene,
building to and around its turning point. 3. Statements and allusions within the lines convey exposition. 4. A unique
verbal style characterizes each role. 5. The flow of progressive beats captivates the reader/audience, carrying them on a
wave of narrative drive, unaware of the passage of time. 6. The language strikes the reader/audience as authentic in its
setting and true to character, thus maintaining belief in the story's fictional reality."

7.5.3 **Desire, scene intention, the beat, the turning point (ch. 12, "Story/Scene/Dialogue").** Desire has five
dimensions: "1. Object of desire 2. Super-intention 3. Motivation 4. Scene intention 5. Background desires". "Scene
intention names what a character immediately wants as a step in the long-term effort of the super-intention"; "If the
writer were to grant the character [the character's] scene intention, the scene would stop" (an interrogation: A wants the
questioning stopped, B wants the secret). Background desires "influence what the character will or will not say".
"every scene contains at least one story value at stake"; "The precise moment in which the charge of one or more values
changes is a scene's turning point"; "A turning point can be created in only one of two ways: by action or revelation";
dialogue "can turn a scene's value charge by action, revelation, or both at once. If a scene has no turning point … then
the scene is merely an exposition-filled nonevent." "The beat is a unit of scene design that contains both an action and
a reaction from someone or something somewhere in the setting"; "each beat tops the previous beat and leads to the next";
"Beats are best identified by gerunds" (insulting/ridiculing, insulting/apologizing …); "The use of gerunds to name the
actions beneath exchanges of dialogue is the best way I know to stop yourself from writing on-the-nose." The five steps
of behavior: "desire to antagonism to choice to action to expression"; "Desire is the source of action, and action is the
source of dialogue"; "A scene lives, not in the activity of talking, but in the action the character takes by talking."
"beat" here is "a unit of action/reaction. An action starts a beat; a corresponding reaction ends it" — not the outline's
turning point nor the script's "(beat)" pause; "No matter how many times a pattern of action/reaction repeats, it
constitutes one, and only one, beat. A scene cannot progress unless its beats change, and beats cannot change until the
characters change their tactics."

7.5.4 **The beat-by-beat parse (Part Four, chs. 13-18).** The method: "separates a scene into its components, pulls out
their subtextual actions, labels them with gerunds, and then discovers how these actions express themselves in dialogue"
(ch. 12). Its form, from ch. 13 ("Balanced Conflict", *The Sopranos*, Character 2 and Character 3): each character's desire,
antagonism, scene intention and tactic stated first; the values that arc ("together/apart …; self-deception/self-awareness
…; and the core value, jeopardy/security") with their opening charges; then the scene "told over thirteen beats", each
beat the lines followed by "ACTION:", "REACTION:" and "SUBTEXT:" (beat 2: "ACTION: [Character 2] propositioning [Character 3]. REACTION:
[Character 3] calling [Character 2] an idiot."); "The first twelve beats match [Character 2's] actions against [Character 3's] reactions, but the last
beat reverses that pattern". Silence can be the reaction: "[Character 3] turns silence into a weapon" (beat 3). The six chapters
are kinds of conflict: balanced, comic, asymmetric, indirect, reflexive, implied/minimal (ch. 12).

7.5.5 **Flaws and fixes (Part Two, chs. 6-9).** Credibility (ch. 6): "Dialogue concentrates meaning; conversation dilutes
it"; empty talk — "one character telling another character something they both already know to satisfy the author's
expositional exigencies"; overly knowing and overly perceptive talk (a character as "a mouthpiece for [the author's] research");
melodrama is "not over-expression but under-motivation". Language (ch. 7): character-neutral language ("Oh my God!") —
"find words so true to your character, they wouldn't quite fit in anyone else's mouth"; "The more emotional people
become, the shorter the words and sentences they use"; "When conflict builds and risk soars, people get emotional,
active, direct, monosyllabic, and dumb"; prefer short speeches; "Eliminate clutter" ("Hi, how ya doing?"); "If your
dialogue does not suggest unsaid thoughts and feelings below its surface, either enrich it or cut it." Content (ch. 8):
"Writing on-the-nose means putting a character's fullest thoughts and deepest emotions directly and fully into what [the character]
says out loud"; "a scene should never be outwardly and entirely about what it seems to be about. Dialogue should imply,
not explain, its subtext"; nonrealist genres are "the great exception"; the monologue fallacy — "any speech that runs on
for too long without change in value charge risks lifelessness"; "The average speaking rate ranges between two to three
words per second", so "a two-minute speech could contain three hundred words"; a long speech is broken "into beats of
action/reaction within [the speaker] and between [the speaker] and the silent Character B"; the "duelogue" (two characters talking
"directly, explicitly, and emotionally about their immediate problem") is fixed by "a third thing that diverts a
duelogue into a trialogue". Design (ch. 9): repetitious beats — "the same charge of value … running on, beat after beat";
mistimed meaning (too late, too soon); mistimed cues — "ideally, the last word or phrase of each speech is the core word
that seals meaning and cues a reaction from the other side of the scene"; misshapen scenes — a turning point "too soon or
too late or not at all"; splintered scenes, among them "The characters' desires run side by side, never crossing in
conflict"; the paraphrasing trap — "Dialogue problems are story problems."

7.5.6 **Each role its own voice (ch. 10, "Character-Specific Dialogue"; ch. 2).** "the more complex the psychology of a
character, the more distinctive [the character's] dialogue must become"; the example contrasts one detective's "abstract,
polysyllabic terms" with the other's "concrete images" (ch. 10). Characterization's third task, "To individualize": "[The character]
speaks like no one we have ever met before." (ch. 2). Stichomythia, "characters alternating single lines or phrases",
named as the origin of rapid exchange (ch. 10).

### 7.6 How the books stand to the web rules of §1-§5

"Confirms" means a book states the same rule; "corrects" means a book states it differently or limits it; "adds" means
a book gives something the web rule lacks. This list only points; the wording is in 7.1-7.5.

- 1.2, 1.3 master shot: Katz confirms (p. 174) and adds that the master may be the only shot, and that the master and the
  wider establishing shot are distinct (glossary, p. 360).
- 1.4 establishing shot: Katz confirms the definition (p. 358); adds that the classic establishing shot "does little to
  raise expectations" (p. 151), that a scene may open on a two-shot and withhold the overview (p. 202), and that a late
  wide shot can be a pause or the last shot (p. 205).
- 1.5 start wide, save the close-up: Katz confirms the direction ("moves in close and does not return to the full shot",
  p. 129); corrects the reflex that an important line needs a close-up ("simply not true", p. 228).
- 1.6 shot / reverse shot: Katz confirms and adds its two advantages (p. 175).
- 1.7 OTS matched: Katz confirms matched pairs in a continuous exchange, adds that mixed pairs can be cut (p. 179), and
  that OTS shots carry a three-player conversation (pp. 197-198).
- 1.8 180-degree rule: Katz confirms (pp. 129-130), adds the pivot shot, reestablishing shot and reuse of setups
  (pp. 135-138), and limits it ("overstated", p. 142; "If it works, use it", p. 187). Murch ranks planarity and 3-D
  continuity last of six, to be given up first ("The Rule of Six").
- 1.9 30-degree rule: no book states it as a rule. Murch adds its reason: a change "neither subtle nor total" (master to
  "ankles up") jars ("Why Do Cuts Work?"). Katz: wide to close-up without a medium shot is accepted today (p. 121).
- 1.10 eyeline match: Katz confirms (p. 123) and adds equal sight-line distance from the lens in alternating close-ups
  (p. 176).
- 1.11 reaction shot: Katz adds "only two types of shots in a conversation, the action and the reaction" (p. 227) and the
  definition (p. 361).

### 7.7 Switching, or instantaneous editing (Zettl ch. 10)

Herbert Zettl, *Television Production Handbook*, 3rd ed., Wadsworth, 1976 ("Zettl"). Scanned with a text layer (OCR).
PDF page = printed + 13 up to p. 439; printed pp. 440-441 are not in this PDF (the scan goes from p. 439 to p. 442);
from p. 442 on, PDF page = printed + 11. Zettl writes for the small-station studio of two or three cameras, a switcher
worked by the technical director (T.D.) on the director's calls, recorded on videotape or sent live.

7.7.1 **Visualization, picturization, the shot, the four transitions.** Visualization is "the framing of a single
shot"; picturization is "the control or the structuring of a shot sequence" (p. 254). "A shot is the smallest
convenient visual unit in film and television. It is generally the interval between two distinct video transitions,
such as cuts, dissolves, or fades." (p. 255, 10.1). The transitions are the cut, the dissolve, the fade and the wipe
(p. 254). The cut "is the most common and least obtrusive transition device"; it serves "clarification and
intensification": "in an interview show, the guest holds up the book [Speaker 2] has written. In order to help the viewer see
better, to identify the title of the book, you cut to a closeup." (p. 255). Five reasons to cut: "(1) To continue
action"; "(2) To reveal detail"; "(3) To change place and time" ("In live television, a cut cannot reveal a change in
time"); "(4) To change impact. A cut to a tighter shot generally intensifies the screen event; a cut to a longer shot
reduces the event impact."; "(5) To establish an event rhythm. … Fast cutting generally gives the impression of
excitement; slow cutting that of calm and tranquility" (pp. 255-256). The dissolve bridges action, marks a change of
place or time or a strong relationship; "Don't overuse them; they create no rhythmic beat." (pp. 256-257). The fade:
"start each program by fading in"; "You should end every program by taking it to black"; going to black before a
commercial is optional; "Be careful not to go to black too often", and the opposite "'never-go-to-black' craze" of
directors who fear the viewer will switch channels (p. 258). The wipe: "Use it sparingly" (p. 259).

7.7.2 **Switching: the decision and the cut at once.** A change of video source while a programme or segment is in progress
"represents instantaneous editing"; "switching in a live situation demands instantaneous decisions. The decision for a
cut and the execution of the cut are virtually simultaneous." (p. 259). The director calls, the T.D. presses: "The
director says, Ready one. … Take one." (p. 266); "Ready two—take two" for a cut, "Ready one for dissolve—dissolve"
(table 15.2, p. 409). The next camera is checked on the preview monitor before it is taken (pp. 263-266). Coming out of
a 58-second film, "an experienced director cuts to 1 at 57, or even 56 seconds, figuring in the delay from signal to
switching execution by the T.D." (p. 248); out of a film with sound the out-cue is the last words: "After "it" you cut to
the next video source, even if your stopwatch may be a second or two over or under" (p. 248).

7.7.3 **Continuity and complexity.** "Continuity editing means the preserving of visual coherence from one shot to the
next; it is basically concerned with the clarification of an event. Complexity editing means the selection and assembly
of shots that primarily, though not exclusively, help to intensify the screen event." (p. 269). "these principles are
not absolutes … depending on the event context or communication aim, some of the dos may easily become the don'ts and
the don'ts the dos of editing." (p. 269).

7.7.4 **Appearance: not too far a jump, not too small a one.** "try to keep the subject recognizable in each shot,
avoiding any extreme changes in distance or angles"; "Cutting from an extreme long shot to an extreme closeup, or from
extreme angles, makes for poor subject continuity." (p. 269). From an extreme long shot to a closeup "we may not be able
to recognize exactly whose closeup it is. You should zoom in somewhat, or cut to a medium shot, before cutting to the
closeup." (p. 270, 10.15). "However, don't cut to nearly identical shots unless you want to repeat a certain picture
sequence for effect"; "the second camera shot should reveal the subject from a slightly different point of view. Why
cut, if you show the viewer exactly the same thing?"; two shots "varying only slightly in viewpoint may produce a jump
cut" (p. 269; glossary, "Jump Cut", p. 514). "If you cannot vary the shot, don't change it. Don't be afraid to stay on
one camera, even for a relatively long period of time." (pp. 269-270). With studio monitors on the floor, when camera 1
has a closeup of a book cover, "camera 2, after seeing this on the studio monitor, can go to a two-shot of the
interviewer and the guest, or to another shot that does not needlessly duplicate camera 1's closeup" (p. 194).

7.7.5 **Location: the line of conversation, and Speaker 1 at one side.** "If, for example, you have two people talking to
each other in an over-the-shoulder two-shot, the viewer expects the people to remain in their relative screen positions
even during closeups or reverse-angle shooting"; "By establishing a vector line—or, as it is also called, "line of
conversation"—and keeping the cameras on one or the other side of it, you will avoid many confusing picturization
mistakes." (pp. 270-271). "For closeups as well as over-the-shoulder shooting, keep both cameras on the same side of the
vector line." (p. 272, 10.18). "In an interview in which a host talks with two or more guests, put the host, not in the
middle, but to one side of the guests. Otherwise you will not be able to maintain proper screen positions." (p. 271).
With Speaker 1 in the middle, "A cut from a three-shot to a two-shot with the guest on screen-left puts the host on the
right side of the screen. A subsequent two-shot with the guest at screen-right will switch the host to screen-left."
(p. 273, 10.19). "By isolating the host at one side of the guests, you will have no trouble maintaining screen
positions. Keep the closeup of the host on the left side of the screen, since we remember [Speaker 1's] screen-left position from
the previous shot." (p. 274, 10.20; its photographs: a three-shot with Speaker 1 at screen-left, Speaker 1's closeup
looking right, a two-shot of the two speakers — read from the figure, paraphrase). Glossary: "Vector Line A dominant
direction established between two people facing each other or through a prominent movement in a specific direction."
(p. 523; also p. 259). The normal camera setup "is from left to right—with camera 1 on the farthest left side of the
action and the highest-number camera on the farthest right side"; crossed cameras complicate the directing (p. 454,
15.35).

7.7.6 **Motion and sound: where in the line the cut falls.** "When cutting on action, cut during the motion of the object
or subject, not before or after it"; a person rising from a chair: "cut to a wider shot just after [the person] has started to
rise but before [the person] stands" (p. 272). A panning shot cuts to a panning shot, not to a still camera (p. 272). A motion
forms a vector line: "don't cut on lateral action from opposite sides" (p. 273). Dialogue: "Sometimes the sound rhythm
dictates certain cutting procedures In an interview, the cut occurs generally at the end of a question or answer, since
cutting at the end of a sentence or phrase produces a cleaner rhythm than in the middle. However, reaction shots are
often smoother when they occur during, rather than at the end of, phrases or sentences. In any case, if someone moves
while in the middle of delivering a speech, cut on the action. Don't wait until the performer has come to an appropriate
point in [the performer's] delivery. Action is usually the stronger motivation for a cut than dialogue." (p. 273; the index's
one entry "Reaction shot, 273" is this paragraph, p. 532). Music: "try to cut with the beat"; with casual or flowing
music, dissolves (pp. 273-274).

7.7.7 **Complexity in switching, and its limit.** The closeup "cannot fail to direct the viewer's attention forcefully to
event detail" and "is an especially effective intensifier if the action remains largely internal, such as in a quandary
of indecision, moments of extreme fear, or the supreme ecstasy of love." (pp. 274-275). A fast zoom-in is "even more
compelling"; "when overdone, the sensitive viewer will lose interest" (p. 275). "Fast cutting that reflects the fast
pace of the event is also a powerful intensifier. Unfortunately, it is very difficult to do in switching. Unless you
have twenty cameras in the field, your cutting will have to slow down after you have cut among the three or four
available cameras, unless you are content to return to the same shots." (p. 276). "In instantaneous editing you usually
try to reflect the action and feeling of the actual event rather than constructing a new one, as in postproduction
editing"; intricate complexity editing belongs in postproduction (p. 276).

7.7.8 **Reaction and cutaway.** In film editing, a statement is spliced "on audio for the beginning of a statement, and
on video for the end of a statement"; a film cut on audio leaves the speaker mouthing a word, so "The next shot should,
therefore, show a reaction that makes some sense, such as a shot of delighted production people hearing the good news."
(p. 281). "On television, reaction is often more effective than action": an explosion suggested by "a closeup of an
actor's face combined with the familiar sound" (p. 297). "Cutaway Shot A shot of an object or event that is peripherally
connected with the overall event and that is neutral as to screen direction (usually straight-on shots). Used to
intercut between two shots in which the screen direction is reversed." (p. 254; glossary p. 509). In a news-film
question-and-answer exchange, the places the speaker refers to are filmed silent afterwards and run as a B-roll over the speaker's continuing sound
(A-B rolling, pp. 282-283).

### 7.8 Directing the studio, the question-and-answer exchange and the panel (Zettl ch. 15; chs. 4, 7, 12-14, 16)

Ch. 15's sections: terminology, timing, visualization and picturization (p. 420), script formats (p. 428), script
preparation (p. 441, of which only the end, p. 442, is in this PDF), rehearsal (p. 442), on-the-air performance
(p. 455). The book has no chapter on such programmes; its question-and-answer exchanges and panels are spread over the chapters cited here.

7.8.1 **The units of a programme, and its time.** "As a director, you are concerned primarily with a four-part division of a
program: (1) the overall show, which is constructed of several segments, (2) the show segment, which consists of two or
more scenes, (3) the scene, which consists of a series of shots, and (4) the shot, which is the smallest workable
program unit." "The shot, as the smallest program unit, has a duration but not a pace." (p. 420, 15.15). "The pace of a
show or a show segment is how fast or how slow the segment or the entire show feels. Show rhythm has to do with the
pacing of each show segment in relation to the next, and to the whole show." (p. 419). Pace rises by speeding the action
or "the delivery of the dialogue", by intensity — "introducing or sharpening some conflict, such as raising the voices of
people arguing" — or by density, "having more things happening within a specific section of running time"; "Fast,
after all, is fast only if we are able to relate the movement to something slower." (p. 420). "there is no mechanical
timing device that will tell you whether an actor races through [the actor's] lines too fast, or whether a whole scene is paced
too slowly" (p. 418). Objective time is clock time and running time; in a 30-minute show the talent gets cues at five,
three, two and one minute, thirty and fifteen seconds before the end, back-timed from the end time (p. 416; glossary
"Time Cues", p. 405); on the air: "Thirty seconds. Wind [the talent] up. Wind [the talent] up. Fifteen … Cut [the talent]." (p. 456). A performer
"must be prepared to fill an extra thirty seconds" or cram material into the last minute (p. 362).

7.8.2 **Visualization and picturization: start from the experience; use the least equipment.** "Directing starts with
visualizing the key images and deciding on where the people and things should be placed relative to the camera, and the
camera relative to the event"; then "the sequence of the events, the picturization", which decides how people move and
"how many cameras you will need" (p. 420). The director starts "with the process message, the experience the percipient
is supposed to undergo during or after the program, and work[s] backward" (pp. 420-421; "process message", p. 383). "In
general, try to get by with as little equipment as possible. If you can do a show with two cameras, don't request
three. Otherwise, you might feel obliged to use the third camera, not because you need it but because it is there";
the beginner "might, for example, be enticed to cut frequently among the three cameras instead of staying on the one
camera that delivers the most expressive picture." (p. 421). "Visualization means seeing single visual images of an
object or event. Picturization means controlling and structuring these images into a shot sequence." (p. 458).

7.8.3 **The two-person question-and-answer exchange, worked through (pp. 421-423).** Process message: "the percipient should gain a deeper
insight into the thinking and feeling and general behavior of the guest." The sample script (15.16, p. 421), video
column / audio column: "CU of host. Host faces camera." / "HOST--INTRODUCES SHOW."; "2-shot of host and guest." /
"HOST--INTRODUCES GUEST."; "Host turns to guest. CU guest." / "HOST--ASKS FIRST QUESTION. GUEST-ANSWERS."; "INTERVIEW:
Favor guest with CU's and XCU's." / "INTERVIEW WITH GREAT EMPHASIS ON WHAT GUEST HAS TO SAY."; "CLOSING: 2-shot of host
and guest. Host faces camera and closes show." / "HOST--MAKES CLOSING REMARKS." Zettl's reading: "the key visualization
should be a closeup of the guest's face. The viewer should not only see the guest but be able, at least to some extent,
to look into [Speaker 2's] personality." "With all due respect to the host, [Speaker 1] simply is not important in this interview.
Therefore, you don't need to show [Speaker 1] at all, except for the opening and closing of the show." (p. 422). One camera is
enough: "you will stay on the guest's face throughout the interview, sometimes viewing it at very close range, and
sometimes from a little farther back"; "the director first reveals the basic relationship of host and guest. They are
obviously sitting next to each other. Then the director concentrates on the guest"; "The host will turn into the camera
for the opening and closing, but [Speaker 1] will face the guest for the rest of the interview. The camera zooms in on the guest
and stays there." (p. 422). Figure 15.17: "Zoom back to 2-shot" for Speaker 2's introduction; "Host turns to guest. Zoom
in to CU of guest."; "During interview stay on guest. Vary between MS and XCU."; closing, "Zoom back to 2-shot. Host
faces camera." (pp. 422-423; the staging drawings put the one camera off Speaker 1's side, facing Speaker 2, and the
speaker's first closeup has the edge of Speaker 1's head in the foreground — read from the figure, paraphrase). "If you
want the host to play a more active role visually, you can maintain the same setup but have the camera zoom back to a
two-shot from time to time, or even arc right, in order to get more of a full-face shot of the host than is possible
from the original camera position." (pp. 422-423).

7.8.4 **The question-and-answer exchange set and its key shots.** Exercise 15.20, "a simple 7-minute interview": "try to visualize some of
your key shots, such as a CU of the guest, an opening two-shot, with the host introducing the guest, an occasional
reaction shot of the host." (p. 426). With the chairs too far apart "a two-shot would be difficult to achieve" — "the
two people would seem glued to the edges of the screen"; "If you shoot from the extreme left, you will get an
over-the-shoulder shot from the host to the guest"; "If you cross-shoot with the other camera, you can get a
reverse-angle shot of the host"; among the fixes, "Use smaller and simpler chairs and place them closer together", a
platform so the cameras need not look down on the performers, the chairs moved away from the background flats
(pp. 426-427). "If you have an interviewer probing tactfully, yet persistently, into the guest's attitudes and feelings,
you don't need a whole living room as a set." "If in an interview I would like the viewer to see intimate closeups
throughout the show, what set do I need?" "Two chairs." (p. 344). Small chairs come "close enough together for intimate
spacing of the two-shot. The edges of the screen seem to attract items within the frame, making them appear farther
apart than they really are." (pp. 353-354). "stage your action for the camera, and not for an audience as you would in
the theater" (p. 424); with one camera, people "move and converse along the z-axis" (p. 424, 15.18). "Always try to
block talent in such a way that the talent rather than your cameras will do most of the moving." (p. 449).

7.8.5 **The panel and Speaker 1 with several speakers.** Speaker 1 sits at one side of the other speakers (7.7.5; pp. 271-274). A
semiscripted panel (15.25, "It's a Matter of Fat", pp. 434-435): the video column pairs every new speaker with a cut to
the speaker's closeup. "Two-shot of [Lead 3] and [Lead 4]" over the two lead speakers' lines; lead speaker [Lead 4] ends that line with the handover
"Isn't that true, [Speaker 9]?", and beside [Speaker 9's] paraphrased answer, "(SAYS THAT THIS IS QUITE TRUE …)", the video
reads "Cut to CU of [Speaker 9]"; then "Cut to CU of [Lead 4]" for Speaker 1's next question, which introduces "four people who can
tell us about their experiences"; then "Cut to CU of [Speaker 10]", "Cut to CU of [Speaker 11]" ("(AGREES WITH [SPEAKER 10] … ELABORATES.)").
A live community programme's show format (15.26a, pp. 436-437): three question-and-answer exchange segments of 8:00, each "INTRO OF GUEST /
DISCUSSION", alternating between two question-and-answer exchange areas and two lead speakers, a super slide with each speaker's name, then a break
of 1:10. The marked format for the rest of such a programme (15.31, p. 445): Speaker 1 "IN PANEL AREA" introduces a film,
then "INTROS SUBJECT FOR DISCUSSION 5:00", then "THROWS TO [HOST 5] WITH QUESTIONS FROM AUDIENCE & PHONES", then "LEADS TO
BREAK". Sound: in a question-and-answer exchange each participant wears that participant's own lavaliere, and lavalieres serve panel shows better than
table microphones (pp. 159-160); table microphones serve "panel shows, public hearings, news shows" (p. 161). In
audience-participation shows the performer walks up to people in the audience with a hand microphone (p. 155); with a
boom, "The sequence of students asking questions is predetermined, or the students asking questions identify themselves
by raising their arms. The person being interviewed can then point out the particular student [Speaker 2] wishes to hear."
(p. 155). The audience's monitors are best turned off when audience members are on camera, "unless you feel that the
embarrassed smiles, gestures of mock horror, or waves to mom at home are essential to the program" (p. 194).

7.8.6 **A multi-camera plan with an audience: the public hearing (ch. 16, pp. 468-470).** A remote, not a multi-camera production, but
the book's one worked camera plan for people talking across a room with an audience present. "You should be able to see
all three supervisors on an LS and get CU's of each one. You should be able to see the witnesses and counselors, in
CU's and LS's. You should also see some of the audience reaction and the workings of the press. This means one camera
looking at the supervisors and one at the witnesses, the counselors, and the audience." (p. 468). "Actually, two cameras
will do"; the direction from witness to supervisor "will represent the line of conversation, the principal vector that
you should not cross with your cameras. If you placed cameras on both sides of it, your screen directions would be
reversed … The supervisors and the witnesses would no longer seem to talk to each other in subsequent closeups, but away
from each other." (p. 469). One camera shoots "over the witnesses"; by zooming, "tight closeups, or … the whole bench in
a long shot" (p. 469). A third camera beside camera 2 "can get reaction shots from the audience and the press, and
relieve camera 2 for closeups or long shots of the witness table" (p. 470).

7.8.7 **Script formats: who writes the camera.** "There are four basic types of script formats: (1) the fully scripted
show, (2) the semiscripted show, (3) the show format, and (4) the fact or rundown sheet." (p. 428). Fully scripted:
"every word that is to be spoken during a show, as well as detailed audio and video instructions" — drama, comedy, news,
most major commercials; "You have definite cue lines, and you know where the camera goes at what time and what shot it
gets", but "if the actor or performer forgets to give you the exact text, your shooting procedure may be seriously
affected." (p. 428). Semiscripted: "the dialogue is indicated but not completely written out. In general, the opening
and closing remarks are fully scripted, while the bulk of the dialogue or commentary is only alluded to, such as: DR.
HYDE TALKS ABOUT NEW EDUCATIONAL IDEAS; DR. WOLFRAM REPLIES."; it "is almost always used for interviews, for programs of
an educational nature, for variety programs, and for other types in which a great amount of ad-lib commentary or
discussion occurs"; "The important part in semiscripting a show is to indicate specific cue lines that tell the
director when to roll a film or when to break the cameras to another set area." (p. 430). Show format: the order of
segments, the set areas, clock and running times; "Most panel discussion shows, or daily interview shows with one
established host and several guests, are directed from a show format." (p. 430). Fact sheet: "Directing solely from a
fact sheet is not recommended. Ad-libbing by both director and talent rarely works out satisfactorily." (p. 430).
Nondramatic scripts have two columns: video on the left, "concerned with what the viewer should see", audio on the
right, "concerned with what the viewer should hear" (pp. 428, 430). "Dramatic shows have a script format all their own
… The exact camera shots are generally left to the director. But the actor's internal and external actions are
generally spelled out, as are the dialogue (upper and lower case) and additional audio material (upper case only)."
(p. 430; example 15.24, pp. 432-433). "Dramatic scripts generally have no audio or video instructions written in. This
way the writer does not dictate the director's specific visualization and picturization processes. You should,
therefore, write the key video instructions into the script yourself. Sometimes it is quite helpful to use small
thumbnail sketches to indicate key visualization or blocking maneuvers." (p. 446, 15.32).

7.8.8 **Marking the script; the shot sheet; the storyboard.** Marking: clear and standard symbols, "Don't overmark your
script", "Place your cue markings before the desired action" (p. 442); before marking, the director should have "the
process message, the key visualization points, and the major picturization processes in your head" (p. 442). The marked
drama (15.32, pp. 446-447) carries camera numbers and framings ("CU", "2-shot", "3-shot", a zoom) in the margin at the
line where each cut falls (read from the figure, paraphrase). The shot sheet "lists every shot a particular camera has to
get"; "Since the director labels all [the director's] shots in consecutive order, regardless of the camera used, the numbers of the
shots for an individual camera are not in consecutive order"; camera 2's sheet: "1 MS of [Actor 2] entering bar. Follow
[Actor 2] through crowded tables." "6 3-shot of [Actor 2], [Actor 1], and waitress. Favor waitress serving at other table." "9
2-shot. [Actor 1] and waitress." (p. 450, 15.34). "Storyboard A series of sketches of the key visualization points of an
event, accompanied by corresponding audio information." (p. 405); it "shows the key visualization points, the major
shots of an event, and gives some idea as to the major picturization" (p. 423).

7.8.9 **Rehearsal.** Script reading: explain the process message, the major actions, and "the performer's relationship to
guests. In an interview, for example, clue in the host to the key questions, what you know about the guest, and the
general tone expected. Usually such talent preparation is done by the producer." (p. 442). For a drama, "An extremely
detailed analysis of the characters is probably the most important aspect" of script reading; the actor must understand
a character so well "that [the actor] is no longer "acting out" the role but living it" (p. 448). Dry run: the actors
block themselves; the director watches "their actions as screen images, not from the point of view of a live audience"
(p. 448). In a small station, rehearsal time is about the programme's running time ("Forty minutes rehearsal time for a
half-hour show is not uncommon"); in the walk-through camera rehearsal "pick the most important parts … Usually these are
the transitions rather than the parts between the transitions." (p. 453). "Don't rehearse right up to air time."
(p. 452).

7.8.10 **On the air; the talent and the camera.** A sample of calls with a performer holding a book (p. 455): "Ready two on
[Performer 1] (the performer). Ready to cue [Performer 1]. Cue [Performer 1], take two. One, get a closeup of the book ([Performer 1] is holding). Ready one,
take one. Two, stay on [Performer 1]." Procedures (p. 456): "2. Cue your talent before you come up on [the talent] with the camera. By the
time [the talent] starts to speak, you will have faded in the picture."; "10. After you have put one camera on the air,
immediately tell the other camera what to do next."; "15. If you rely heavily on postproduction editing, get some
cutaways."; "16. During the show, speak only when necessary." "even if you are videotaping the show, you should always
act as though it were going on the air at the same time" (p. 455). The talent (ch. 13): a performer "speaks directly to
the camera or communicates with other performers or the … audience" (p. 358); "you must look directly into the lens
whenever you intend to establish eye contact with your viewer" (p. 359); with two cameras the performer follows the
floor manager's cue or the tally light to the camera on the air, without jerking from one to the other (p. 360); in
"lectures, demonstrations, interviews" there is no detailed blocking, so the talent warns the crew before an
unrehearsed move (pp. 360-361). The television actor plays to the lens: "your reactions are equally as effective on
television as your actions" (p. 369).

7.8.11 **Television framing (ch. 4).** On the small screen "you have to operate more with closeups (CU) and medium shots
(MS) than with long shots (LS) and extreme long shots (XLS)" (p. 72). Field of view in five steps — extreme long shot,
long shot, medium shot, closeup, extreme closeup — "relative" designations (p. 72, 4.14), plus bust shot, knee shot,
two-shot, three-shot and over-the-shoulder shot (p. 73, 4.15). A speaker talking into the camera goes to screen centre;
a person looking to the side goes off-centre — "The more profile the performer turns, the more space you must leave in
front of [the performer]. This space is generally called "nose room."" (p. 74); "You must leave some headroom" (p. 78); cutoff lines
(neck, shoulders) not on the frame edge (p. 77); "Try to avoid odd juxtapositions of performers with background
objects, such as giving the appearance of plants growing out of people's heads." (p. 80). Glossary: "Cover Shot
Wide-angle shot giving basic orientation of place and action; covers a great area." (p. 508); "Establishing Shot
Orientation shot, usually a long shot." (p. 511).
